# Photo rulebook

For Antonio. Adopted October 24, 2026.

This rulebook sets the quality bar for Cookbook photographs and teaches the reasons behind it. The [scorecard](photo-scorecard.md) turns it into a keep, edit, or retake decision for each photo.

The [photography plan](recipe-photography.md) covers workflow and publishing. The [shot list](recipe-shot-list.md) says what to photograph for each dish. This document says how, and why.

Each rule has three parts:

- **Rule.** What to do.
- **Why.** What goes wrong otherwise. Learn this part; it lets you break a rule on purpose.
- **Practice.** A short drill. Most take 15 minutes with an egg, a banana, or a plate of rice.

A phone and a window are the whole kit. Nothing here requires new equipment.

## 1. Frame for the site

**Rule.** Hold the phone horizontally. Keep the defining part of the dish inside the middle band of the frame, away from the top and bottom fifths.

```
+--------------------------------------------------+
|  top ~18 %: cut off on the wide recipe cover     |
|--------------------------------------------------|
|                                                  |
|   SAFE BAND                                      |
|   The part that makes this dish recognisable     |
|   goes here: the pegao, the crust, the crumb.    |
|                                                  |
|--------------------------------------------------|
|  bottom ~18 %: cut off on the wide cover  [EN/ES]|
+--------------------------------------------------+
            4:3 landscape: the card shows all of it
```

**Why.** One file appears as a 4:3 card, a 2.1:1 cover on a desktop, a 1.9:1 cover on a phone, and a 1.91:1 social preview. All of them crop from the centre. A landscape photo loses only its top and bottom edges. A portrait photo loses almost two thirds of its height on the cover, which is what happened to both live covers. The [audit](photo-audit-2026-10.md) has the measurements.

The card is also small: about 314 px wide in the desktop grid. At that size, only shape, colour, and contrast survive. A photo must read as "fried chicken" or "rice" at the size of a postage stamp.

**Practice: the crop test.** Pick five food photos already in your library. In Photos, crop a copy of each to 4:3, then to a very wide 2:1. Shrink the window until the photo is about 8 cm (3 in) wide. Ask: can I still tell what dish it is? What did the wide crop take away?

## 2. Light

### 2.1 Use one soft light from the side

**Rule.** Put the dish near a window that is not in direct sun. Light should come from the left, the right, or behind the dish at an angle. Turn off the kitchen lights.

**Why.** Light direction decides where shadows fall, and shadows are how a photograph shows texture. Side light skims across the surface, so every grain of rice and every crumb of breading casts a small shadow. Light from directly above, like a ceiling fixture, fills those shadows in and the food looks flat. A large, close light source, like a window, makes shadow edges soft. A small or distant one, like a bulb or the sun, makes them hard and black.

**Practice: the window clock.** Put a plate with one egg or a slice of bread on a table beside a window. Photograph it from four positions, walking around the table so the window is to your left, behind the plate, to your right, and behind you. Lay the four photos side by side. Ask: in which one does the surface look most three-dimensional? In which does it look flat? Which one has your own shadow in it?

### 2.2 Light from behind for shine and liquids

**Rule.** For glossy sauces, soups, and drinks, put the window behind the dish and slightly to one side, at about ten or two o'clock.

**Why.** Shine is a reflection of the light source. Light from behind reflects toward the camera, so oil, glaze, and broth glisten and drinks glow. Light from in front reflects away and the same food looks dry.

### 2.3 Soften the shadows with a white card

**Rule.** If the shadow side is too dark, stand a sheet of white paper or foam board on that side, just outside the frame. Move it closer for lighter shadows.

**Why.** The card bounces window light back into the shadows. It changes contrast without adding a second light colour. Distance controls strength: light falls off quickly, so a few centimetres make a visible difference.

**Practice: the fill card.** Set up as in the window clock with the window to one side. Take three photos: no card, card at 50 cm, card at 15 cm. Ask: which shadow looks most like what your eye saw?

### 2.4 Never mix light colours, never use flash

**Rule.** Use one kind of light per photo. No flash, no overhead kitchen light alongside a window, no light from the range hood.

**Why.** Daylight is blue compared with most indoor bulbs, which are orange. The camera can correct one colour cast across the whole frame. It cannot correct two at once, so a mixed photo has blue shadows and orange highlights that no edit fixes. Flash is a small, hard light from the camera's own position: it flattens texture, makes glare on sauces, and throws a black shadow behind the plate.

**Practice: colour temperature.** At night, photograph a white plate under the kitchen lights. The next day, photograph it beside the window with the kitchen lights on, then off. Compare the plate's white in all three.

## 3. Exposure

**Rule.** Tap the food to focus, then drag the sun icon down slightly so the brightest part of the food keeps its detail. Check rice, sugar, cream, and white plates.

**Why.** A camera sensor records a limited range of brightness. Anything brighter than the top of that range becomes pure white with no detail, called clipping, and no edit can bring it back. Shadows that are a little dark still hold detail that an edit can lift. So when in doubt, expose a little darker.

**Practice: the exposure ladder.** Photograph a bowl of white rice or a dusted cupcake five times: drag exposure down two steps, down one, normal, up one, up two. Zoom in on the brightest area of each. Pick the brightest photo where you can still see individual grains or sugar crystals. That is your exposure.

## 4. Colour

**Rule.** Whites should look white: plates, rice, linen. Food colour should match what is on the table. Do not add saturation, filters, or warmth to make food look richer.

**Why.** The eye judges colour by comparison. If the plate is yellow, the eye reads the whole dish as stale. If the plate is neutral, the eye trusts the food's colour. The site also applies its own warm, muted filter to every photo for now (see the [audit](photo-audit-2026-10.md#4-a-shared-filter-changes-every-photograph-on-the-site)). A file that is already warm becomes muddy under it.

**Practice: find neutral.** Open a food photo with a white plate in Photos on the Mac. In Edit, open White Balance, choose Neutral Gray, and click the plate. Toggle the change on and off. Ask: which version would you rather eat?

## 5. Angle

**Rule.** Choose the angle that shows the dish's defining feature.

| Dish shape                | Angle                                         | Shows                               | Cookbook examples                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Flat, or in a wide vessel | Overhead, camera parallel to the table        | Pattern, colour, what is in the pot | Arroz con gandules, arroz con pollo, asopao, habichuelas, broccoli, guacamole toast, sofrito, lamb chops with vegetables |
| Has height or a cut face  | About 45°, roughly the view of someone seated | Crust, thickness, the inside        | Flank steak slices, chicken thighs and drumsticks, sea bass, arepas, brownies, banana bread with a cut slice             |
| Tall or stacked           | Straight on, 0° to 20°                        | Layers, height, the side of a glass | A tall pancake stack, a single cupcake in its liner                                                                      |

The [shot list](recipe-shot-list.md) names the angle for each dish that needs a cover. It uses 45° for coquito and the pancakes, so the surface of the drink and the browned pancake tops show as well as the sides. That is the trade-off to understand: 45° shows some of the top and some of the side; straight on shows the whole side and none of the top.

**Why.** Each angle hides something. Overhead hides height, so a stack of pancakes becomes one circle. Straight on hides the surface, so a bowl of soup becomes a bowl. The in-between angle of someone standing at the counter, about 60°, usually shows neither the top nor the side well; it is the most common angle in phone food photos and the most common reason they look casual.

**Practice: three angles.** Photograph one dish overhead, at 45°, and straight on. Ask for each: what is this angle hiding? Which one would make someone who has never eaten this dish understand it?

## 6. Lens, focus, and geometry

### 6.1 Use the 1× or 2× lens, never 0.5×

**Rule.** For 45° and straight-on photos, step back and use 2×. For overhead, 1× is fine. Never use 0.5×.

**Why.** A wide lens held close stretches whatever is nearest the camera. Round plates become ovals, the front of the dish swells, and glasses lean. Stepping back and zooming in keeps proportions natural.

**Practice: the lens test.** Frame the same plate to the same size three ways: 0.5× close, 1× middle, 2× far. Look at the plate's outline and the rim of a glass.

### 6.2 Focus on the front of the food

**Rule.** Tap the nearest edge of the main food to focus. Do not use Portrait mode.

**Why.** The eye goes first to the sharpest part of a photo. The front edge of the food is where the viewer expects detail. Portrait mode fakes background blur with software, and it often blurs edges of food or plates by mistake.

### 6.3 Keep it level

**Rule.** Turn on the grid and level in the Camera settings. Overhead, line up the two crosshairs before shooting. At 45°, keep the table edge parallel to the frame or clearly diagonal, not almost straight.

**Why.** An overhead photo that is slightly tilted makes round plates look oval and makes the table appear to slope. An almost-straight line reads as a mistake; a clearly diagonal one reads as a choice.

## 7. Composition

**Rule.** Let the dish fill most of the frame, about half to four fifths of the 4:3 card. Put the most important part in the safe band. Either keep the plate fully inside the frame or crop into it decisively; do not clip a thin sliver of its edge.

**Why.** At card size, a small dish surrounded by table is a small brown shape. Filling the frame gives the eye the texture it came for. A sliver of a cut plate edge reads as a framing accident and pulls the eye to the border.

Useful patterns:

- **Centre for overhead.** A round plate centred overhead is calm and survives every crop.
- **Thirds for 45°.** Place the main food on one third and let a secondary element, a fork or the pot, sit on another.
- **Odd numbers.** Three tenders or five slices look arranged; two or four look like a pair or a grid.
- **Lead in.** A spoon handle or the edge of a cutting board can point into the dish.

**Practice: fill the frame.** Photograph one plate from far away, then step closer three times until the food reaches the frame edges. Do the crop test from section 1 on all four.

## 8. Background and props

**Rule.** Use a small, repeatable set: two surfaces, three plates or bowls, one linen. Matte, plain, and neutral. Props must belong to the recipe: the caldero, the cast iron skillet, the spoon you serve with.

**Why.** Pattern competes with food for attention. A strongly patterned plate makes the food's texture harder to read, and at card size the pattern wins. Glossy surfaces reflect the window as white streaks. A repeated set makes 28 covers look like one collection in the grid, and the vessel from the recipe tells the story better than a borrowed prop.

Build the reference set before the first session:

- Surfaces: one wood board or table, one plain light surface such as a linen or matte board.
- Plates and bowls: plain, matte, white, cream, or one muted colour. One shallow bowl for asopao and habichuelas.
- Linen: one neutral cloth.
- Vessels: whatever the recipe cooks in.

Packaging, bottles with labels, phones, mail, and unrelated objects stay out of frame.

**Practice: background swap.** Put the same plate of food on three surfaces: a patterned cloth, a wood board, and a plain light surface. Look at them at card size. Ask: where does your eye land first in each?

## 9. Styling

**Rule.** Photograph the dish within three minutes of plating. Wipe plate rims. Show a real portion with real imperfections. Show the inside when the inside is the point. Add nothing the recipe does not contain.

**Why.** Food changes fast. Steam disappears, sauces skin over, greens wilt, and fried food loses its shine in minutes. Smears on a rim read as carelessness, but a few crumbs beside a sliced loaf read as real. Cutting a brownie, splitting an arepa, or lifting a spoon of rice shows the texture a reader wants to cook toward. Accuracy is the house rule: a garnish that is not in the recipe teaches the reader the wrong dish.

Dish-specific cues:

- Rice: separate grains, and the pegao in a detail shot.
- Stews and soups: broth surface showing, not drowned under garnish.
- Fried and roasted: crust in side light, shot immediately.
- Baking: one cut or opened portion beside whole ones.
- Drinks: a full glass in back light; the bottle only as support.

**Practice: the three-minute window.** Plate something hot. Photograph it at 0, 3, and 8 minutes without moving the phone. Compare.

## 10. Editing

**Rule.** Edit the full-resolution original in Photos, in this order, and stop as soon as it looks like the table did.

1. Crop to 4:3 landscape and straighten.
2. Exposure, then Highlights down if white food has lost detail.
3. Shadows up slightly if needed.
4. White Balance: Neutral Gray on a white plate, or Warmth and Tint by eye.
5. Contrast: small changes only.

Do not use filters, Vignette, or Brilliance above a small amount. Keep Saturation and Vibrance between 0 and +10. Keep the unedited original.

**Why.** Each step depends on the one before. Crop first so you judge only what will be seen. Fix brightness before colour, because colour looks different at different brightness. Filters and heavy saturation push every photo in a different direction, which breaks the collection. Small edits keep the food honest.

**Practice: undo test.** Edit one photo, then press and hold the image to compare with the original. If you cannot tell which version looks like the food you ate, back off the edit.

## 11. Privacy

**Rule.** No faces. No children in the frame, including hands. No screens, mail, documents, addresses, or window views that identify the house. Remove location data on export.

**Why.** The repository and the site are public, and a published image stays findable after it is deleted. Location metadata in a photo can reveal where the family lives.

## 12. Process photographs

Process photos follow the same rules, with two differences. They go inside the method step they explain, and they must show a cue a cook can act on: the rice shining in sofrito, the dough's consistency, the colour of brown butter. If the photo does not answer "is mine ready?", it does not need to be on the page.

## Practice path

Do the drills roughly in this order. Each builds on the one before.

1. The window clock (2.1): light direction.
2. The fill card (2.3): contrast.
3. Colour temperature (2.4): mixed light.
4. The exposure ladder (3): highlights.
5. Three angles (5): choosing an angle.
6. The lens test (6.1): distortion.
7. Background swap (8): attention.
8. Fill the frame and the crop test (7, 1): composition for this site.
9. The three-minute window (9): timing.
10. Grade yourself: score one of your own recent food photos with the [scorecard](photo-scorecard.md). Reshoot the same dish to fix its two lowest scores, then score again.

Keep the drill photos in an album. Looking back at the first and last drill after a month is the clearest way to see what you have learned.

## Glossary

- **Clipping.** Highlights so bright they record as pure white with no detail.
- **Colour cast.** An overall tint, often yellow from bulbs or blue from shade.
- **Diffuse light.** Light from a large source, which makes soft shadow edges.
- **Fill.** Light bounced into shadows to lighten them.
- **Keystone.** Lines that should be parallel converge because the camera is tilted.
- **Safe band.** The middle of the frame that every site crop keeps.
- **Specular highlight.** A small bright reflection of the light source on a shiny surface. Small ones are fine and show gloss.
